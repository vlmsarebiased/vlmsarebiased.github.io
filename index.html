TL;DR: State-of-the-art VLMs know that the Adidas logo has 3 stripes and a dog has 4 legs, scoring 100% on the originals. On counterfactual images, where we add a 4th stripe or a 5th leg, their counting accuracy falls to 17.05%. Most wrong answers are the memorized answer, not what is in the image.

Featured on Sky News

AI could be giving US lethal edge in Iran war, but there are dangers

Sky News used our counterfactual “chicken” test in a film and article on the role of AI in modern warfare and its dangers.

A dog with five legs

Show a VLM a photo of a dog and ask how many legs it has. Every model we tested answers 4, and is right. Now edit the photo so the dog has a fifth leg and ask again. The models still answer 4.

They are not counting. They recognize a dog, recall that dogs have four legs, and report that. The same happens with a 3-legged chicken, an Adidas logo with a fourth stripe, a US flag with 14 stripes, and a chess board missing one piece.

Language models are known to be swayed by what they memorized from the Internet. Earlier evidence that VLMs share this problem came from yes/no questions that state the biased fact in the prompt, such as “Is the mouse smaller than the cat?” next to an image where the cat is smaller. That leaves open how much the image matters, and whether the bias shows up when the question is neutral. We ask plain, objective counting questions and let the image do the work.

How we test it with VLMBias

For each well-known subject, we change its signature element, for example giving the Adidas logo a fourth stripe, and ask the model to count.

  • Sanity check. “What shoe logo is this?” → Adidas. “How many stripes?” → 3. All five models score 100% on the originals.
  • Counting (Q1, Q2). Two neutral phrasings, such as “How many legs does this animal have?” and “Count the legs of this animal.”
  • Identification (Q3). “Is this an animal with 4 legs?” The correct answer on a counterfactual is No.

VLMBias covers 7 domains in decreasing order of popularity. They are animals, logos, flags, chess pieces, game boards, optical illusions, and patterned grids. The last one is a pattern we designed from scratch, so it cannot be on the Internet. Animal and logo images are photo-realistic and made with image editors, while the other five are drawn with code. Humans review every generated image and reject any that are low-quality or debatable.

We call an answer biased when it matches the common-knowledge value, such as 3 stripes for Adidas, which is wrong for the counterfactual. Our main experiments cover five models, three thinking models (Gemini-2.5 Pro, o3, o4-mini) and two non-thinking models (Sonnet-3.7, GPT-4.1).

    Finding 2 · Paper, Sec. 4.3

    The errors follow memory, not the image

    If models were simply bad at counting, their mistakes would be scattered. They are not. In fact, 75.70% of all answers on counterfactual images are exactly the biased, memorized value.

    A direct yes/no question confirms it. Asked “Is this an animal with 4 legs?”, every model is 100% correct on the originals but only 25.11% correct on the counterfactuals. Most of the time they answer Yes to a five-legged animal. They do not register that the subject has changed.

    Finding 3 · Paper, Sec. 4.4

    The background sets the trap

    What in the image triggers the memorized answer? We removed everything except the element to be counted, such as the camel's legs without its body, the shoe without the player, and the flag's stars without the blue field.

    Accuracy more than doubles, from 17.05% to 38.14%, and the bias rate drops from 75.70% to 35.12%. The context that tells the model “this is a dog” or “this is the EU flag” is what sets it up to answer from memory. It also suggests that a VLM able to crop accurately would count much better.

    Finding 4 · Paper, Sec. 4.5

    Thinking longer helps, until it doesn't

    Thinking models barely beat non-thinking ones overall, so we looked at accuracy as a function of how long they reason. For Grok-4, o3, and o4-mini, accuracy rises with more reasoning tokens to a ceiling of about 40%, then falls. Past that point, overthinking hurts. The bias rate keeps dropping the longer they think.

    Overconfidence gets in the way. o3 skips reasoning entirely on 36.1% of questions. o4-mini with tools (zoom, crop, Python) improves steadily the longer it works, but it chooses to use those tools on only 29.66% of questions, so its overall gain is just 1.9 points (23.18% → 25.08%).

    Finding 5 · Paper, Sec. A.9, A.10

    Better prompts barely help, and a label makes it worse

    We tried two helpful instructions.

    • Debiased. “Do not assume from prior knowledge and answer only based on what is visible in the image.”
    • Double-check. After the first answer, we send “Please double-check your answer and give your final answer in curly brackets, following the format above.”

    They raise accuracy by only 1.87 and 2.70 points. Going the other way, writing the subject's name into the image, such as “Ebbinghaus illusion” or “Adidas”, lowers accuracy by 4.49 points. Thinking models are hit hardest. o4-mini drops 6.56 points and o3 drops 6.41, against 2.81 for Sonnet-3.7 and 2.67 for GPT-4.1.

    New results · Oct 2026

    GPT-6 Astra is much better, but still biased

    We ran the full benchmark (2,784 questions) on GPT-6 Astra with medium reasoning effort. It is a big jump. Astra reaches 84.10% mean accuracy across the 7 tasks, up from 17.05% for the 2025 models in Table 1. But counting a zebra's legs or the stripes on a shoe is something a child can do by pointing and counting, and Astra still gets about one question in six wrong. When it is wrong, 92.4% of the time it gives the biased, memorized answer.

    The mistakes cluster where the prior is strongest. On car logos Astra is right only 16.7% of the time and gives the brand's real count 82.6% of the time. It also gets worse as the image gets smaller, from 92.67% at 1152px to 85.34% at 768px and 73.49% at 384px. In our human study, people counted counterfactual legs correctly 93.75% of the time after looking for just 2 seconds, while Astra gets 83.70% on animals.

    Why? It often answers without thinking

    Astra decides for itself how long to reason. On 31% of questions (867 of 2,784) it uses zero reasoning tokens, and those answers are much worse, with 71.28% accuracy and 28.72% biased, against 89.51% and 8.71% when it reasons. On logos, every answer given without reasoning is wrong, and on animals accuracy falls from 93.89% to 40.38%. And as with the thinking models in Finding 4, reasoning for very long hurts again.

      Video models · Oct 2025

      Can video generation models count?

      We gave Sora 2 two of our counterfactual images, a chicken with three legs and a zebra with five, and asked it to “have a man (or woman) hold a picture of this animal and explicitly count how many legs it has.”

      The person in the video gets the count wrong in every clip. Most of the time the problem is where they point. Their finger lands on the wrong places instead of on each leg, and the count drifts back to the familiar number, two legs for a chicken and four for a zebra.

      The two clips with Sam Altman’s Sora cameo go one step further. Sora quietly redraws our picture as a normal animal, a chicken with two legs and a zebra with four, and he still miscounts or points at the wrong legs. In one he counts four legs on a two-legged chicken. Turn the sound on.

      More in the paper

      The paper appendix has many more experiments. Four of them help explain where the bias comes from and what does not have it.

      Paper, Sec. A.8

      The vision encoder sees the extra leg

      We train linear probes on LLaVA-OneVision's internal features to tell 4-legged from 5-legged animals. The probe on the vision encoder is 95.26% accurate, and probes on later layers still reach about 90%. Yet the full model answers correctly only 49.71% of the time, near chance, and gives the biased answer 99.43% of the time. The leg count is in the model's features, but the language model overrides it.

      Humans do not have this problem

      Paper, Sec. A.22

      Even side by side with the original, they miss the change

      We showed each model the original animal and our counterfactual next to each other and asked “Compare the two images side by side. Do the animals in image 1 and image 2 have the same number of legs?” The correct answer is always No. The models say No only 11.76% of the time, far below the 50% a coin flip would get. With the normal animal right there for reference, they still do not see the extra leg.

      Also in the appendix

      • Re-running experiments multiple times gives consistent results (Sec. A.11).
      • In-context examples of animals with abnormal legs fail for o4-mini, which sometimes distrusts the labels (Sec. A.12).
      • Models fail the same way whether or not they come from the family that generated the images, which rules out generation bias (Sec. A.17).
      • Image resolution has little effect, with 15.43% at 384px vs. 18.28% at 1152px (Sec. A.18).
      • Locate-then-count prompting does not significantly improve accuracy (Sec. A.20).
      • Adding the subject name to the text prompt lowers accuracy further (Sec. A.21).

      Conclusion

      State-of-the-art VLMs recognize familiar subjects perfectly, and that knowledge is exactly what makes them fail when the subject is changed. On VLMBias they reach 17.05% counting accuracy and give the memorized answer 75.70% of the time, across thinking and non-thinking models alike. The background context drives much of this, and neither prompting nor longer reasoning removes it. VLMBias offers a simple, objective way to measure this failure, and a human-supervised pipeline for building more tests like it.
